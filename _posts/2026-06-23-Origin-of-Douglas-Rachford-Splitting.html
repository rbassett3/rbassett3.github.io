On the Origins of Douglas-Rachford Splitting

Robert Bassett

Abstract

In this document I will introduce the original application of Douglas Rachford (DR) splitting and derive the connection between the original and conventional forms of DR splitting used today.

Introduction

The Douglas Rachford splitting algorithm for solving \[\min_{x \in \mathcal{X}} f_{1}(x) + f_{2}(x)\] consists of the following iteration.

  1. \(k = 0\), \(x^{0} \in \mathcal{X}\) some initial point, and \(\lambda > 0\).

  2. \(y_{1} = \mathrm{Prox}_{\lambda f_{1}} (x^{k})\)

  3. \(y_2 = \mathrm{Prox}_{\lambda f_{2}}(2 y_{1} - x^{k})\)

  4. \(x^{k+1} = x^{k} + y_{2} - y_{1}\)

  5. \(k = k+1\), repeat steps 1-4.

For the remainder of this document, we will use the language of resolvents instead of proximal operators. Recall the resolvent of a (potentially set-valued) maximal monotone operator \(A: \mathcal{X} \rightrightarrows \mathcal{X}\) is the (provably single-valued) operator \(J_{A}: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{X}\) defined by \(J_{A}(v) = (I + A)^{-1} v\). Recall that the proximal operator of a closed convex function is the resolvent of its subdifferential. Call the subdifferentials of \(f_{1}\) and \(f_{2}\) \(B\) and \(A\), respectively (note the change of order here–this is for the sake of connecting our notation to that of the original sources).

By eliminating \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{2}\) from the Douglas Rachford algorithm, the iteration becomes \[x^{k+1} = x^{k} + J_{\lambda A} (2 J_{\lambda B} (x^{k}) - x^{k}) - J_{\lambda B} x^{k}.\] Simplifying, \[\label{DR-conv} x^{k+1} = J_{\lambda A} (2 J_{\lambda B} - I)x^{k} + (I- J_{\lambda B}) x^{k}.\] The iteration [DR-conv] is the conventional/modern form of DR splitting.

This modern form of DR splitting was first introduced in . Notably, this not the original paper by Douglas and Rachford. In my assessment, the DR splitting could more reasonably be called the Lions Mercier splitting because these authors discovered the method as a general purpose tool. In the original paper by Douglas and Rachford , the authors focus exclusively on finite difference methods for certain partial differential equations, solving the heat equation and Poisson equation in two and three dimensions. The proofs of these results are detailed analyses of the spectra of certain finite difference operators, so that the generalization in Lions & Mercier is a significant achievement.

The modern form of DR splitting [DR-conv] is due to Lions & Mercier. Though Douglas and Rachford did not introduce it as such, Lions & Mercier note that the splitting introduced by Douglas and Rachford had the form \[\label{DR-og} u^{k+1} = (I + \lambda B)^{-1}\left[(I + \lambda A)^{-1} ( I - \lambda B) + \lambda B \right] u^{k}.\] One advantage of the original form [DR-og] is that it provides a more intuitive connection to backward/implicit Euler discretization. Indeed, the update [DR-og] can be written as the pair of updates \[\begin{aligned} u^{k+1/2} &= \left[(I + \lambda A)^{-1}(I - \lambda B) + \lambda B\right] u^{k} \label{DR-part1}\\ u^{k+1} &= (I + \lambda B)^{-1} u^{k+1/2} \label{DR-part2} \end{aligned}\]

Rewriting, [DR-part1]-[DR-part2] can be written \[\begin{aligned} \frac{u^{k+1/2} - u^{k}}{\lambda} &= - A u^{k+1/2} + \lambda A B u^{k} \label{DR-rewrite-part1}\\ \frac{u^{k+1} - u^{k+1/2}}{\lambda} &= - B u^{k+1},\label{DR-rewrite-part2} \end{aligned}\] from which we can see that the original DR splitting [DR-og] is almost a repeated implicit Euler scheme in \(A\) and \(B\), with the only discrepancy the \(\lambda A B u^{k}\) term in [DR-rewrite-part1]. Finally, note that by eliminating \(u^{k+1/2}\) via [DR-rewrite-part2], [DR-rewrite-part1] becomes \[\frac{u^{k+1} + \lambda B u^{k+1} - u^{k}}{\lambda} = - A(u^{k+1} + \lambda B u^{k+1}) + \lambda A B u^{k}.\] Simplifying, this becomes \[\frac{u^{k+1} - u^{k}}{\lambda} + \lambda A B u^{k+1} - \lambda A B u^{k} = - Bu^{k+1} - A u ^{k+1}, \label{DR_og_simple}\] which will be useful in the sequel.

Equivalence Between Modern and Original DR Iterations

We next show that the iterations [DR-og] and [DR-conv] are equivalent, as suggested in . Manipulating the iteration [DR-conv] using the definition of the resolvent, we have \[\begin{aligned} x^{k+1} &= J_{\lambda A} (2 J_{\lambda B} - I)x^{k} + (I- J_{\lambda B}) x^{k}\\ &=(I + \lambda A)^{-1}(2 (I + \lambda B)^{-1} - I) x^{k} + (I - (I + \lambda B)^{-1}) x^{k}\\ &= 2 ( I + \lambda A)^{-1}(I + \lambda B)^{-1} x^{k} - (I + \lambda A)^{-1} x^{k} + x^{k} - (I + \lambda B)^{-1} x^{k}. \end{aligned}\] Perform the substitution \(u^{k} = (I + \lambda B)^{-1} x^{k}\). Then we have \[u^{k+1} = (I + \lambda B)^{-1} \left[ (I + \lambda A)^{-1} u^{k} - (I + \lambda A)^{-1} \lambda B u^{k} + \lambda B u^{k} \right],\] which shows that the iteration [DR-og] is equivalent to [DR-conv] under the substitution \(u^{k} = (I + \lambda B)^{-1} x^{k}\)

How DR Splitting Was Originally Applied

The original DR paper applied splitting to a few different PDEs. We’ll focus on the 2D Poisson equation. Let \(\nabla^{2}_{x}\) and \(\nabla^{2}_{y}\) be finite difference operators on a rectangular grid \(R\) with boundary \(S\). Each operator \(\nabla^{2}_{x}\) and \(\nabla^{2}_{y}\) operates along rows or columns of the grid, so that solutions of equations involving one of these operators to be solved as a 1D problem. We want to solve the problem \[\begin{aligned} \nabla^{2}_{x} w + \nabla^{2}_y w &= f \quad \text{ on } R \label{pde}\\ w &= u \quad \text{ on } S, \end{aligned}\] where \(w = u\) gives the boundary condition. We will split the PDE [pde]. Let \(A(w) = -\nabla^2_{x} w+ f\) and \(B(w) = -\nabla^{2}_{y} w\). Plugging these into the iteration [DR_og_simple], we have \[\frac{u^{k+1} - u^{k}}{\lambda} + \lambda \nabla^{2}_{x} \nabla^{2}_{y} (u^{k+1} - u^{k}) = \nabla^{2}_{y} u^{k+1} + \nabla^{2}_{x} u^{k+1} - f\]

A Final Interesting Note

The original Douglas Rachford paper considered the heat equation, a PDE which is not steady-state (i.e. it has a \(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}\) term). The Lions Mercier paper also considers this case, in Section 2 as what it calls “evolution equations”. There is a surprising result: when the \(\lambda\) parameter decreases linearly with \(n\), an extension of the Douglas Rachford algorithm converges to a solution of these problems. For fixed \(\lambda\) the authors show that DR converges to the solution of a steady-state/stationary problem with no \(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}\) term. This is an interesting feature of DR splitting of which I was not aware.
